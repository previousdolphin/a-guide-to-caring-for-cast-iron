A field guide to bare cast iron

The soap argument is over.

Every kitchen has a self-appointed cast iron enforcer who lunges for the bottle of dish soap the second it comes near the good skillet. They mean well. They're also decades out of date. Here's the care routine that actually holds up — built on what seasoning is chemically doing, not on what your grandfather insisted.

The verdict, for the impatient

Soap will not strip a properly seasoned cast iron pan. Modern dish soap is a mild surfactant built to cut grease off dishes without touching the pan underneath — it is not the harsh, lye-heavy soap earlier generations had actual reason to fear. What ruins cast iron is neglect: standing water, air-drying, or years without a whisper of oil. Wash it like you mean it. Dry it like you're in a hurry. Oil it like you respect it. That's the whole argument, settled.

01 — Origins

Where the myth came from

The soap rule isn't nonsense — it's just retired. Through most of the 19th and early 20th centuries, household soap was made at home or bought as a hard cake of lye and rendered fat, sometimes cooked in a cast iron pot itself. Lye is genuinely aggressive: given enough scrubbing over enough years, it will saponify the oil sitting on a pan's surface — the same reaction that turns fat into soap in the first place. A pan washed daily in true lye soap could, eventually, lose its seasoning.

Dish soap changed. Synthetic detergents replaced saponified fat by the mid-20th century, built to bind to grease and rinse it away rather than react with the oil chemically. That's a different substance doing a different job. The old warning kept circulating anyway, the way kitchen folklore does — passed from cook to cook long after the thing it warned against had left the shelf.

Meanwhile the actual threats to a pan — standing water in the sink, a forgotten trip through the dishwasher, or storage bone-dry with no oil at all — got a pass, because they're less satisfying to warn someone about than "don't you dare use soap."

02 — Routine

The daily method

Six steps, five minutes, no drama. Do this every time you cook and your seasoning improves — it doesn't wear down.

  1. 1

    Clean it while it's warm

    A cooling pan releases food far more easily than a cold one. Deal with it right after you eat, not after the dishes have piled up in the sink.

  2. 2

    Scrape before you scrub

    A pan scraper, chainmail pad, or a stiff brush will lift stuck-on bits without needing steel wool or muscle. Do this under warm running water.

  3. 3

    Add soap if you actually need it

    Cooked fish, or anything greasy enough that water alone won't cut it, gets a small amount of dish soap and a non-abrasive brush or sponge. This is the step people are afraid of. It is fine.

  4. 4

    Dry it properly

    This is the step people skip, and the one that actually matters. Towel it off, then set it on a warm burner for a minute until every trace of water is gone. Air-drying on a rack is how rust starts.

  5. 5

    Oil it while it's still warm

    About half a teaspoon of a neutral, high-smoke-point oil, wiped over the interior and rim with a paper towel — then wipe most of it back off. You want a whisper, not a puddle. Oily to the touch means too much.

  6. 6

    Store it somewhere dry

    A cabinet, not next to the sink where steam collects. If you stack pans, a folded paper towel between them stops moisture getting trapped against the surface.

03 — Chemistry

Building and maintaining seasoning

What seasoning actually is

Seasoning isn't a layer of old grease, and it isn't dirt. It's polymerized oil — a thin film of fat heated past its smoke point until its molecules link together into a hard, plastic-like coating bonded to the iron. That's what makes cast iron naturally non-stick, and it's also why soap can't touch it: soap breaks down loose fat and grease, not a cross-linked polymer fused to metal.

Seasoning a bare or stripped pan

  1. Wash it, then dry it completely, including a short stint on the stove.
  2. Rub in the thinnest possible layer of neutral oil — grapeseed, canola or refined vegetable oil all work. Wipe off far more than feels reasonable; a glossy, wet-looking coat turns gummy, not hard.
  3. Place it upside down in an oven at 450–500°F (230–260°C), with foil or a tray on the rack below to catch drips.
  4. Bake for an hour, then let it cool in the closed oven.
  5. Repeat three to six times. Each coat should look thin, matte, and slightly darker than the last.

Everyday maintenance

You don't need to formally "reseason" after every wash. Every time you cook with a bit of fat and dry the pan properly afterward, you're adding another microscopic coat. A pan in regular use gets better for years; one left in a drawer slowly loses ground to humidity — not to soap.

04 — On the record

Myth vs. fact

MythFact
"Soap ruins the seasoning." Modern surfactant-based soap doesn't react with a polymerized oil coating. It never did the damage it's blamed for.
"Never let it touch water." Brief contact while washing is fine. Standing water — in a sink, a dishwasher, or on a drying rack — is the actual enemy.
"Flaxseed oil gives a glass-hard finish." Flaxseed forms a beautiful but brittle coat prone to flaking. A neutral, high-smoke-point oil builds a more durable, forgiving layer.
"Never cook tomatoes or wine in it." A well-seasoned pan tolerates normal acidic cooking fine. Hours of simmering acidic sauce can dull seasoning — it won't ruin the pan outright.
"An inherited rusty pan is junk." Rust is cosmetic and fully reversible: steel wool, a wash, a full dry, and a fresh round of seasoning brings it right back.

05 — Fixes

Troubleshooting

Rust spots have appeared

Scrub the affected area with steel wool or coarse salt and a little oil until the rust is gone and you're back to grey metal. Wash, dry thoroughly on the stove, then reseason the whole pan with a thin coat of oil baked as usual.

The surface feels sticky or tacky

Too much oil was applied and it didn't fully polymerize. Strip the tacky patch with a scrub and hot water if needed, then reseason with a much thinner layer — wipe off far more than feels reasonable before it goes in the oven.

Food keeps sticking

Usually the pan wasn't hot enough before food went in, or the seasoning is still young. Preheat properly, use enough fat for the cook, and give it time — seasoning improves with every use, not overnight.

There's a metallic smell or taste

Common on new or recently stripped pans with thin seasoning. Cook a few fattier, oil-heavy meals in a row to build the layer up faster, and dry-and-oil after each one.

The seasoning is cracking or flaking

Often a sign of flaxseed oil or coats applied too thick. Strip back to bare metal with oven cleaner or a vinegar soak, then rebuild with several thin coats of a neutral, high-smoke-point oil instead.

06 — Quick answers

Frequently asked

Can it go in the dishwasher?

No — not because of the detergent, but because of the extended soak and heat-drying cycle. Standing hot water for an hour is a much bigger threat than a squirt of soap ever was.

Can I use metal utensils on it?

Yes. Properly built seasoning is tougher than people assume — a metal spatula won't gouge it. Just avoid aggressive scraping on a still-thin, newly seasoned pan.

Does this apply to enameled cast iron too?

No — enameled cast iron has no bare metal to season and follows its own care instructions from the manufacturer. This guide covers bare, traditionally seasoned cast iron only.

Is it safe to cook eggs in it yet?

Once the seasoning is well established, yes — eggs are actually a good test of how your seasoning is coming along. If they stick badly, give it more oil, more heat control, and more time.

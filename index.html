Field guide·Bare cast iron·12 min read

Every kitchen has a self-appointed cast iron enforcer who lunges for the dish soap the second it comes near the good skillet. They mean well. They’re also decades out of date. This is the care routine that actually holds up — built on what seasoning is chemically doing, not on what your grandfather insisted.

Here’s what you can expect once you stop treating the pan like a museum piece:

Soap. Soap will not strip a properly seasoned pan. Modern dish soap is a mild surfactant built to lift grease off a surface — not the harsh, lye-heavy bar earlier generations had real reason to fear.

Water. Brief contact while washing is fine. Standing water — the overnight soak, the dishwasher, the drying rack — is what actually starts rust.

Oil. A half-teaspoon after each wash, wiped back until the pan looks dry. Every cook is a coat; the surface gets better for years instead of wearing down.

Time. Five minutes, start to finish. Wash it like you mean it. Dry it like you’re in a hurry. Oil it like you respect it. That’s the whole argument, settled.

Where the myth came from

The soap rule isn’t nonsense — it’s just retired. Through most of the 19th and early 20th centuries, household soap was made at home or bought as a hard cake of lye and rendered fat, sometimes cooked in a cast iron pot itself. Lye is genuinely aggressive: given enough scrubbing over enough years, it will saponify the oil sitting on a pan’s surface — the same reaction that turns fat into soap in the first place.

Dish soap changed. Synthetic detergents replaced saponified fat by the mid-20th century, built to bind to grease and rinse it away rather than react with the oil chemically. That’s a different substance doing a different job. The old warning kept circulating anyway, passed from cook to cook long after the thing it warned against had left the shelf.

  1. 1800sLye soap. Homemade bars of lye and rendered fat. Scrubbed daily for years, it really could eat into seasoning.
  2. 1930s–50sSynthetic detergents. Surfactants that lift grease without reacting with it take over the kitchen sink.
  3. 1970sThe dishwasher. Long, hot soaks and heated drying cycles — the real threat nobody warned about.
  4. TodayThe folklore persists. “No soap” outlives the soap it was about. Rust and neglect get a pass.

Meanwhile the actual threats to a pan — standing water in the sink, a forgotten trip through the dishwasher, storage bone-dry with no oil at all — got a pass, because they’re less satisfying to warn someone about than “don’t you dare use soap.”

The daily method

Six steps, five minutes, no drama. Do this every time you cook and your seasoning improves — it doesn’t wear down.

  1. 01

    Clean it while it’s warm

    A cooling pan releases food far more easily than a cold one. Deal with it right after you eat, not after the dishes have piled up.

  2. 02

    Scrape before you scrub

    A pan scraper, chainmail pad or stiff brush lifts stuck-on bits without steel wool or muscle. Do it under warm running water.

  3. 03

    Add soap if you need it

    Fish, or anything greasy enough that water won’t cut it, gets a small amount of dish soap and a non-abrasive sponge. This is the step people fear. It is fine.

  4. 04

    Dry it properly

    The step people skip, and the one that matters. Towel it off, then a minute on a warm burner until every trace of water is gone.

  5. 05

    Oil it while it’s warm

    About half a teaspoon of neutral, high-smoke-point oil, wiped over the interior and rim — then wipe most of it back off. Oily to the touch means too much.

  6. 06

    Store it somewhere dry

    A cabinet, not beside the sink where steam collects. Stacking? A folded paper towel between pans stops moisture getting trapped.

The difference between the old habit and the method is small on paper. On the pan, over a year, it’s the difference between a slick black surface and a rusty ring. Toggle between the two:

Seasoning

What it actually is. Seasoning isn’t a layer of old grease, and it isn’t dirt. It’s polymerized oil — a thin film of fat heated past its smoke point until its molecules link into a hard, plastic-like coating bonded to the iron. That’s what makes cast iron naturally non-stick, and it’s why soap can’t touch it: soap breaks down loose fat and grease, not a cross-linked polymer fused to metal.

Which oil. Almost any fat will polymerize in a 450–500°F oven — seasoning deliberately goes past the smoke point. What differs is the finish. Neutral, refined oils build a durable, forgiving film; flaxseed builds a glassy one that tends to flake.

Iron isn’t special per gram — it’s the thickness. A 4–5 mm wall stores more heat than any thin pan, so a steak doesn’t crash the temperature. The trade-off is low conductivity: preheat for five to ten minutes so the hot spot over the burner spreads to the rim.

Seasoning a bare or stripped pan

  1. Wash it, then dry it completely, including a short stint on the stove.
  2. Rub in the thinnest possible layer of neutral oil — grapeseed, canola or refined vegetable oil all work. Wipe off far more than feels reasonable; a glossy, wet-looking coat turns gummy, not hard.
  3. Place it upside down in an oven at 450–500°F (230–260°C), with foil or a tray on the rack below to catch drips.
  4. Bake for an hour, then let it cool in the closed oven.
  5. Repeat three to six times. Each coat should look thin, matte, and slightly darker than the last.

Everyday maintenance. You don’t need to formally “reseason” after every wash. Every time you cook with a little fat and dry the pan properly afterward, you add another microscopic coat. A pan in regular use gets better for years; one left in a drawer slowly loses ground to humidity — not to soap.

Myth vs. fact

Five rules people repeat at the stove, and what’s actually going on.

The mythWhat’s true
“Soap ruins the seasoning.”Modern surfactant-based soap doesn’t react with a polymerized oil coating. It never did the damage it’s blamed for.
“Never let it touch water.”Brief contact while washing is fine. Standing water — in a sink, a dishwasher, or on a drying rack — is the actual enemy.
“Flaxseed oil gives a glass-hard finish.”Flaxseed forms a beautiful but brittle coat prone to flaking. A neutral, high-smoke-point oil builds a more durable layer.
“Never cook tomatoes or wine in it.”A well-seasoned pan handles normal acidic cooking fine. Hours of simmering acidic sauce can dull seasoning — it won’t ruin the pan.
“An inherited rusty pan is junk.”Rust is cosmetic and reversible: steel wool, a wash, a full dry, and a fresh round of seasoning brings it right back.

Troubleshooting

Most problems come from the same three causes: too much oil, not enough heat, or water left sitting. Here’s how we’d rule on a few classic household arguments:

“

It was in my grandmother’s garage for twenty years. It’s orange. My uncle says throw it out.

Ruling

Keep it. Rust is surface oxidation, not a death sentence. Scrub back to grey metal with steel wool, wash with soap, dry on the burner, and season three to six coats from scratch. Old pans often have smoother, machined cooking surfaces — worth the afternoon.

“

My roommate soaks it in the sink overnight and swears it’s fine because they never use soap.

Ruling

The roommate has it backwards. Hours of standing water are what cause rust; a squirt of soap was never the problem. If there are spots, scrub them out, dry, oil and bake one coat. Then reassign sink duty.

“

I did six coats of flaxseed like the internet said. Now black flakes are coming off into my food.

Ruling

Flaxseed forms a hard but brittle film, and thick coats make it worse. Strip back to bare metal with oven cleaner or a short vinegar soak, then rebuild with thin coats of a neutral oil. The flakes are unappetizing but harmless.

“

Everyone says cast iron is non-stick. My eggs weld themselves to it every single time.

Ruling

Usually the pan wasn’t hot enough, or it was too hot. Preheat on medium for five minutes, add a real pat of fat, and wait for it to shimmer before the eggs go in. Young seasoning improves with every cook — give it a few weeks.

Quick fixes

Rust spots have appeared

Scrub the area with steel wool or coarse salt and a little oil until you’re back to grey metal. Wash, dry thoroughly on the stove, then reseason the whole pan with a thin coat of oil baked as usual.

The surface feels sticky or tacky

Too much oil was applied and it didn’t fully polymerize. Scrub the tacky patch with hot water, then reseason with a much thinner layer — wipe off far more than feels reasonable before it goes in the oven.

Food keeps sticking

Usually the pan wasn’t hot enough before food went in, or the seasoning is still young. Preheat properly, use enough fat, and give it time — seasoning improves with every use.

There’s a metallic smell or taste

Common on new or recently stripped pans with thin seasoning. Cook a few fattier meals in a row to build the layer up faster, and dry-and-oil after each one.

The seasoning is cracking or flaking

Often a sign of flaxseed oil or coats applied too thick. Strip back to bare metal, then rebuild with several thin coats of a neutral, high-smoke-point oil.

Quick answers

Can it go in the dishwasher?

No — not because of the detergent, but because of the long soak and heated drying cycle. An hour of hot standing water is a much bigger threat than a squirt of soap ever was.

Can I use metal utensils on it?

Yes. Properly built seasoning is tougher than people assume — a metal spatula won’t gouge it. Just go easy on a still-thin, newly seasoned pan.

Does this apply to enameled cast iron?

No — enameled cast iron has no bare metal to season and follows its own care instructions from the manufacturer. This guide covers bare, traditionally seasoned cast iron only.

Is it safe to cook eggs in it yet?

Once the seasoning is established, yes — eggs are a good test of how it’s coming along. If they stick badly, give it more fat, better heat control, and more time.
